# Does a low-stakes simulator help you get good at setting boundaries?

**Question under test.** A friend argued that a text-based social simulator *"will not be
efficient because there are no actual stakes involved."* The counter-claim: for a person who
engineered their life around conflict-avoidance and has a reflex of *swallowing*
boundary-crossings, a stakes-free simulation could **bootstrap the brain to start spotting such
situations** and help develop the ability to handle them with real people. This document tests
both the exercise (is rehearsal without stakes useful?) and the goal (is *"getting good at
setting boundaries"* the right target, or is the real thing broader?).

Scope note: the peer-reviewed evidence below concerns **assertiveness training, behavioral
rehearsal, and virtual-reality exposure** — human role-play with a clinician, or VR with
physiological presence. Our grove is a **text chat graded by an LLM**. That is a weaker form of
the thing the evidence supports, and the gap is called out explicitly in §4. Read this as
"the mechanism is real and well-evidenced; our implementation is the lightest, least-validated
point on that spectrum."

---

## Bottom line

- **The friend is partly right and partly wrong.** He is right that *stakes (arousal) are what
  drives transfer to real life* — the strongest transfer evidence (VR exposure) works precisely
  because it manufactures arousal. He is wrong that a stakes-free drill is therefore
  "inefficient": rehearsal is the **validated first stage** of every evidence-based skills
  protocol. It is cheap, and it does the one thing the "swallower" most needs first — build
  *recognition* and a *pre-loaded response*.
- **The user's bootstrapping claim is the stronger reading of the mechanism**, but with a
  precise boundary: the simulator reliably builds the *skill you don't yet have or can't
  retrieve* (acquisition). It does **not**, on its own, inoculate you against freezing under
  real arousal (performance). Closing that second gap requires graded escalation and,
  eventually, real practice. A stakes-free drill is a **scaffold, not a substitute.**
- **The goal is set correctly but under-specified.** "Setting boundaries" is the *observable
  behavior*. It sits on top of a broader, well-validated clinical target — **assertiveness** —
  which reliably co-moves with social anxiety, self-esteem, and well-being. For the specific
  profile described (chronic appeasing), the deeper levers are **self-efficacy** ("I can do
  this") and **arousal tolerance**, not just the verbal script. The topic *is* broader than
  boundaries; boundaries is its most measurable surface.

---

## 1. The discriminator: acquisition deficit vs. performance deficit

This is the single most useful distinction for settling the argument, and both sides are
implicitly talking about different halves of it.

- A **skill-acquisition deficit** = the behavior isn't in your repertoire (you don't know what
  to say, or don't even register that a line was crossed).
- A **skill-performance deficit** = the behavior *is* in your repertoire but you fail to emit it
  in the moment — typically because "excessive anxiety during social interaction consumes
  limited attentional resources, leaving less cognitive capacity for skillful interaction."
  Studies of shy individuals find they have **equal knowledge** of appropriate social behavior
  as non-shy peers, but are "less likely to respond with these behaviors" and less likely to
  *believe they could* (University of Maryland thesis on social-skills vs. performance deficits;
  Gumpel, *Psychology in the Schools*, 2007).

**Why this settles the debate:**
- The user's *bootstrapping / recognition* argument is an **acquisition** argument — and for
  acquisition, low-stakes rehearsal is exactly the right, evidence-backed tool (§2).
- The friend's *"no stakes"* critique is really a **performance** argument — arousal is what
  breaks the skill in the wild, and a calm text chat doesn't rehearse under arousal (§3).
- The person who habitually *swallows* boundary-crossings usually has **both**: no pre-loaded
  response (acquisition) *and* a freeze/appease reflex under pressure (performance). A simulator
  attacks the first cleanly and the second only weakly.

---

## 2. Evidence that rehearsal — even without stakes — builds the skill

**Behavioral rehearsal is a load-bearing, validated component, not filler.** The evidence-based
skill-building loop is **instruction → modeling → rehearsal → feedback**: you're told the
principle, shown it, you practice it, and you get corrective feedback. Removing any element
degrades it; the rehearsal + feedback pair is what converts "knowing" into "doing." In a
randomized trial training clinicians, adding behavioral rehearsal (expert demonstration then
role-play) produced **large competence gains over instruction alone (d ≈ 1.23–1.40)**
(Damen et al., *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 2025).

**Assertiveness training specifically has a real, if under-cited, evidence base.** Speed,
Goldstein & Goldfried's review, *Assertiveness Training: A Forgotten Evidence-Based Treatment*
(*Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice*, 2018), argues assertiveness is a **transdiagnostic**
factor tied to a wide range of clinical problems, and that assertiveness training — built on
modeling, behavioral rehearsal, and role-play — is a valuable, and neglected, stand-alone
intervention.

**A modern RCT confirms the gains are large and durable.** The Swedish *Respekt2* trial (210
adults; Björkstrand et al., transdiagnostic CBT for assertiveness, *PMC10235435*) is the
strongest single data point here:
- Large gains in adaptive assertiveness vs. wait-list (Rathus Assertiveness Schedule
  **ES ≈ 1.02–1.24**; Adaptive Assertiveness **ES ≈ 0.95–1.00**).
- **Reduced *aggressive* assertiveness** (ES ≈ −0.62 to −0.72) — i.e., it trains *calibration*,
  not just "be louder."
- Gains **held at 1-year follow-up** (ES rose to ≈ 1.30–1.73), and **spilled over** into lower
  social anxiety (ES ≈ 0.59–0.93) and higher well-being (ES ≈ 0.70–1.05).
- Caveat matching our situation: outcomes were **self-report scales**, not observed behavior or
  partner ratings — so "transfer to real interaction" is inferred, not directly measured.

*(Note: the popular term "fawn" / "people-pleasing" — Pete Walker's coinage — is clinical folk
language, not an academic construct. The research-side equivalents are **sociotropy** and
**submissive/subordinate behavior**. Treat "fawn" as a useful label, not as science.)*

---

## 3. Evidence on transfer — and where the friend is right

**Simulated practice *can* transfer to the real world — and the mechanism is arousal/presence,
which is the friend's exact point.** Virtual-reality exposure therapy (VRET) is the cleanest
evidence that rehearsing a threat in a synthetic environment reduces real fear of it. In a
self-guided VRET pilot for public-speaking anxiety (Kahlon et al., *Frontiers in Psychiatry*,
2021, n=32), two 1-hour sessions of speaking to a virtual audience produced:
- Large drops in avoidance (89.7 → 47.5 on 0–100) and speech-anxiety cognitions, held at
  1-month follow-up;
- A **measurable physiological response** — heart rate fell ~4.5 bpm within sessions — i.e., the
  body treated it as real enough to habituate.

The authors state plainly that "virtual exposure … translates to 'real life' threat." **But note
what makes VRET work: presence and arousal.** The whole reason VR outperforms imagining is that
it *manufactures stakes*. So the strongest transfer evidence in the literature **vindicates the
friend's intuition**: the closer the practice gets to real arousal, the more it transfers.

**Role-play's known limitation is exactly ecological validity.** Across psychology, "a primary
limitation of role-play is questionable criterion validity — behavior in a role-play may not
represent behavior in a natural situation." Skills practiced in a safe setting don't
automatically generalize; generalization has to be *engineered* (graded difficulty, varied
contexts, real reps). This is why every serious protocol ends rehearsal with **graded exposure
to the real thing** — rehearsal opens the door, it doesn't walk you through it.

## 4. The honest gap: this evidence vs. *this* simulator

The evidence above is about **human role-play with clinician feedback** or **VR with
physiological presence.** Our grove is **text chat graded by an LLM on "subtext / leverage /
thermodynamics."** Two caveats follow, and they are the sharpest, most on-topic ones:

1. **The grader's validity is itself unvalidated.** All of the efficacy evidence assumes
   *accurate corrective feedback* (the "F" in the rehearsal loop). If the LLM mis-grades
   frame — rewards a clever line that would land badly, or flags a healthy boundary as
   "needy" — the drill can train the wrong reflex. Feedback quality is the mechanism; it can't
   be assumed, it has to be checked.
2. **Text with no arousal is the weakest point on the spectrum.** It is strong for **acquisition**
   (recognition, pre-loading responses) and weak for **performance** (the freeze happens under
   bodily arousal a chat window never induces). This is not a reason to abandon it — it's a
   reason to treat it as **stage one** and be explicit that stages two and three (graded
   pressure, then real reps) live outside the text box.

Stated fairly: **the mechanism the simulator relies on is real and well-evidenced; our
implementation sits at the lightest-weight, least-validated end of it.** That is fine for a
bootstrap. It is not a finished intervention, and the doc should not pretend otherwise.

---

## 5. Is the goal set correctly?

**Yes as a target, but it's the tip of a larger construct.**

- **"Setting boundaries" is the observable behavior.** It's measurable (you either held the line
  or you didn't), which makes it a good *scoring surface* for a drill. Keep it.
- **The validated construct underneath it is assertiveness** — and the RCT evidence shows
  assertiveness is *transdiagnostic*: move it and social anxiety, self-esteem, and well-being
  move with it (§2). So the work is bigger than "saying no"; boundaries is just its most
  legible face.
- **For the specific "swallower" profile, the true bottleneck is usually not the words.** It's
  (a) **self-efficacy** — the shy/appeasing person often *knows* the assertive move but doesn't
  *believe they can execute it* (§1), and (b) **arousal tolerance** — staying regulated while
  the other person pushes back. A drill that only grades the *script* trains the least-broken
  part. The higher-leverage targets are "did you notice the crossing in real time?" and "could
  you stay regulated while holding?"

**Reframe the goal as a ladder, not a point:**
1. **Notice** the boundary-crossing in the moment (recognition — the user's bootstrapping claim,
   and the thing text chat is genuinely good at).
2. **Have a response pre-loaded** (acquisition — behavioral rehearsal, well-evidenced).
3. **Stay regulated while delivering it under push-back** (performance / arousal tolerance —
   needs escalating stakes; text is weak here).
4. **Do it with a real person and survive the discomfort** (transfer — needs real reps; outside
   the simulator).

The current grove nails rungs 1–2. Its design ceiling is rungs 3–4, and that ceiling is the
"stakes" the friend was pointing at.

---

## 6. Practical implications for the grove

If the aim is to stay aligned with the evidence (not required, but this is what the research
implies):
- **Guard the feedback signal.** The LLM grader is the "F" in the rehearsal loop; spot-check its
  gradings against the principles so it isn't reinforcing the wrong reflex (§4.1).
- **Build in graded escalation.** The evidence-based path is easy → hard, and the drill's own
  design already escalates — lean into it as the primary lever for approaching "stakes."
- **Score recognition, not just the reply.** Add "did you flag that a boundary was crossed?" as
  a dimension — that's rung 1, the highest-yield rung for this profile.
- **Name the hand-off to reality.** Position the simulator honestly as a *bootstrap for rungs
  1–2*, with an explicit prompt to attempt small real-world reps — that's where transfer is
  actually won.
